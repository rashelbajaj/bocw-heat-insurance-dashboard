Parametric Insurance — Research Prototype

BOCW Parametric Heat Insurance

A model of automatic, satellite-triggered heat-stress payouts for India's registered construction workers — using the existing BOCW cess fund.

What this is

When a district's daily maximum temperature crosses its own historical 95th-percentile threshold for 2 consecutive days, every registered BOCW worker in that district auto-receives Rs 330 (~half a day's wage). If it crosses the 97th percentile, Rs 655 (full day's wage). No claims filing — payouts trigger automatically from ERA5 satellite temperature data and route through the existing BOCW Aadhaar/DBT rails. This dashboard tests whether the scheme is affordable from existing BOCW cess balances under a range of scenarios, modeled on 10 heat seasons (2015–2024).

Stock vs Flow — definitional note distinguishing cumulative reserves from annual obligations

Two distinct quantities in this analysis are measured in the same units (Rs Cr) and require separation:

STOCK — cumulative reserve
Rs 65,749 Cr
Total BOCW cess balance across 32 state boards as of 2024-07-01. Accumulated since 1996 inception of the BOCW Welfare Cess Act under a 1% levy on construction expenditure. A cumulative reserve, not an annual cost.
Appears in: "Cess allocation available," "Cess balance," solvency comparisons.
FLOW — annual obligation
Rs 6,776 – 15,730 Cr / year
Annual payout — amount owed by the scheme in a single year. Rs 6,776 Cr is the 10-year empirical mean (2015–2024); Rs 15,730 Cr is the worst observed year (2024).
Appears in: "Annual payout," "Expected payout per year," "Worst single year," VaR quantiles.
Coverage relationship: at the national aggregate, the stock (Rs 65,749 Cr) represents approximately 4.2× the worst observed annual flow (Rs 15,730 Cr) and 9.7× the mean annual flow. State-level concentration, however, drives the policy-relevant constraint: Uttar Pradesh alone accounted for 41.4% of the 2024 national payout, and its own cess balance does not cover that obligation. The Solvency view presents these classifications state-by-state rather than as a national aggregate.

Interpretive note: Rs 65,749 Cr is the cumulative reserve pool, not a single-year payout. Single-year payouts in the empirical record range from Rs 560 Cr (mildest, 2021) to Rs 15,730 Cr (worst, 2024).

How to read this dashboard before looking at any numbers, read this

Every number on this dashboard comes from one dataset: ten heat seasons of daily district-level maximum temperatures (2015–2024), derived from ERA5 reanalysis. What differs across tabs is how that 10-year dataset is summarized:

  • Worst-year payout — payouts computed from a single year's observed temperatures, taken as the maximum across the 10-year empirical record (2024 in the current dataset).
  • 10-year total — sum of all ten annual payouts (2015 + 2016 + ... + 2024). Useful for cumulative fiscal impact.
  • Annual average / expected — 10-year total ÷ 10. The steady-state cost expected in a typical year under the empirical distribution.
  • YoY payouts tab — presents each of the ten years separately in a table and chart, allowing comparison of mild years (e.g., 2016–17) against extreme years (2024).
  • Monte Carlo tab — resamples those ten annual totals 5,000× (slider-adjustable) to simulate synthetic 10-year futures → produces P95 / P99 tail estimates.
  • Cap-hit analysis — uses all ~7,020 district-years (702 districts × 10 yrs) to count how often the seasonal cap bites.

Summary: a single-year snapshot (2024) represents the worst-observed stress-test benchmark. This dashboard reports that single-year value alongside the distribution across the 10-year record, presenting both the stress-test benchmark and the steady-state expectation.

At a glance headline numbers — live, updates with sliders/scenario

What the scheme is

A parametric heat-insurance product for India's ~5.64 crore (56.4 M) registered BOCW construction workers (as of 2024-07-01, RS Session 265 AU 411) across 32 states/UTs. When a worker's home district exceeds its own historical 95th-percentile max temperature for 2 consecutive days, every worker in that district automatically receives Rs 330 (~$3.90) — roughly half a day's wage. If it crosses the 97th percentile, they receive Rs 655 (~$7.70), a full day's wage. A per-worker seasonal cap (Rs 4,600 / $54 at 7-day, Rs 6,550 / $77 at 10-day) prevents runaway payouts in extreme years. No claims filing, no adjuster visit — the payout is triggered automatically by ERA5 temperature data, and paid via the existing BOCW Aadhaar/DBT rails. The fund can be paid from the existing BOCW cess (~Rs 65,749 Cr cumulative reserve balance, as of 2024-07-01), from worker + cess + commercial insurer risk-transfer, from GoI/State general revenue, or from a donor/DFI grant — all five scenarios are toggleable in the dashboard.

Every scenario delivers the same Rs 330 / Rs 655 payout to the worker when a heat event triggers. What differs is the funding source and who bears the year-to-year variance risk. Multi-year benchmark comparison what if the "stress year" isn't 2024?

2024 was the hottest year in the observed record — approximately 1.4× the second-worst year (2019, Rs 10,908 Cr) and approximately 28× the mildest year (2021, Rs 560 Cr). Anchoring solvency tests to 2024 is defensible as a climate-resilience stress test, though some stakeholders may argue 2024 is unrepresentative. To address this, the scheme is re-scored against five different benchmark years for sensitivity analysis.

HOW TO READ EACH ROW
Benchmark = the stress year against which the scheme is evaluated.
National payout = what the scheme would have paid out in that year, across all 32 states.
vs 2024 = how much smaller/bigger this payout is compared to the 2024 headline.
Solvent / Tight / Insolvent = of the 32 states, how many can cover the payout from their cess allocation. ✓ = covered comfortably · ! = covers but tight · ✗ = cannot cover.
UP share = how much of the national payout comes from Uttar Pradesh alone. Higher = more UP-concentrated stress.
Moving down the table, benchmark years become milder, payouts shrink, and more states classify as solvent.
How to use this in presentation: Sensitivity analysis across multiple benchmarks: under the 2019 (2nd-worst observed) or 9-year average excluding 2024 benchmarks, the scheme exhibits positive coverage across the majority of state boards. Under the 10-year mean, the scheme aligns with expected-loss cost. The 2024 figure represents a stress-test ceiling rather than a forecast.

Glossary — what every term means

TermPlain-English meaning
Parametric insuranceInsurance that pays automatically when a measured trigger (e.g. temperature) crosses a threshold. No claims-filing, no adjuster, no proof-of-loss. Contrast with indemnity insurance, which pays based on actual measured loss after an inspection.
TriggerThe event that starts a payout. Here: district max temp ≥ 95th (or 97th) percentile for 2 consecutive days.
Percentile thresholdDistrict-specific cutoff, calibrated to its own 10-year history. Jaisalmer's 95th pct is much hotter than Shimla's 95th — triggers are climate-adaptive, not absolute.
Normal / Severe tierTwo-tier payout: ≥ 95th pct = Rs 330 (normal, ~½ day wage), ≥ 97th pct = Rs 655 (severe, full day wage). Mutually exclusive — a severe event doesn't also pay normal.
Seasonal capMaximum a single worker can receive per heat season. 7-day cap = Rs 4,600, 10-day cap = Rs 6,550. Prevents runaway payouts in an extreme year like 2024.
District-yearOne district observed in one calendar year. 702 districts × 10 years = 7,020 district-years in the sample. Used to report cap-hit frequencies.
BOCW cessBuilding and Other Construction Workers' cess — a 1% levy on all building construction cost in India, collected by state labour boards under the 1996 BOCW Act. ~Rs 65,749 Cr balance sits across 32 state BOCW funds. Restricted to worker welfare by law.
Cess allocation %The share of cess that the BOCW board is willing to commit to heat insurance (vs. pensions, skill training, etc.). Dashboard default is 20%. Not a policy today — a scenario.
Cess CAGR (growth rate)Compound Annual Growth Rate of the cess balance. The empirical CAGR between the 2022 and 2024 RS snapshots is 16.25%; 6% is applied as the forward assumption in the default scenario. The 16% figure reflects accumulation from past construction-sector activity and is descriptive, not projective.
Expected annual payoutMean payout per year based on the 2015–2024 empirical record. The steady-state cost expectation.
Worst-year payoutMaximum single-year payout in the 2015–2024 record. Used as the stress-test benchmark for solvency in this analysis.
VaR (Value at Risk) — P95 / P99The payout level exceeded with probability 5% (P95) or 1% (P99), estimated from a Monte Carlo resample of the 10-year historical distribution. Corresponds approximately to "1-in-20-year" and "1-in-100-year" payouts respectively.
Monte Carlo bootstrapGenerates synthetic 10-year futures (default 5,000 iterations, slider-adjustable 500–20,000) by resampling observed state-year totals with replacement. Captures observed variance; does not capture climate-change drift — see Limitations tab.
Coverage ratio(Cess allocation) ÷ (worst-year payout). Expressed in plain English in the Solvency view as "Covers X% — short Rs Y Cr" or "Covers X% — surplus Rs Y Cr". A state is classified solvent if the ratio is ≥ 1.0 (i.e., allocation fully covers the worst observed year).
Solvent / Tight / InsolventSolvent: cess allocation is well above worst year. Tight: can cover, but >80% of allocation is spent in the worst year. Insolvent: worst-year payout exceeds cess allocation — board would need top-up or bigger allocation.
Commercial loadingWhat a commercial insurer charges on top of expected payout. The "expected loss" (EL) is the math-fair price; a commercial insurer charges EL × loading-multiplier to cover cost of capital, variance risk, admin, reinsurance, profit margin, and GST. The dashboard default is 2.0× (slider-adjustable 1.0–3.0×); 2.0–2.5× is typical for parametric weather products in emerging markets.
Worker premiumThe contribution a single registered worker pays annually to enroll in the parametric heat-insurance product. Sidebar slider adjusts between Rs 0 and Rs 500 per worker per year (default: Rs 150/worker/year, equivalent to ~Rs 12.50/month). A small but non-zero stake signals participation and reduces adverse selection.
Basis riskThe divergence between what the parametric trigger measures (district-level maximum temperature) and the actual heat exposure of an individual worker. A night-shift worker, or one operating in an air-conditioned interior, may receive payment despite limited exposure; conversely, an outdoor worker in a district that fell marginally below the threshold receives nothing. Typical parametric products accept 10–20% basis risk.
Risk transferPaying a commercial insurer a fixed premium so they absorb the year-to-year variance. Converts a variable cost into a fixed cost. The scheme pays more on average (because of loading) but is predictable.
Reinsurance layeringThe commercial insurer sells off the extreme tail (e.g. payouts beyond P99) to a global reinsurer. Lowers their capital requirement and therefore the loading charged to BOCW.
DBT (Direct Benefit Transfer)India's Aadhaar-linked bank-account system. BOCW already uses it to pay pensions; heat-insurance payouts would use the same rails for <72hr turnaround.
Worker allocation: UniformSidebar toggle. Each district in a state is assigned an equal share of the state's registered workers (e.g., Rajasthan 3.32M workers ÷ 33 districts ≈ 100,624 per district). A simplifying assumption — see Limitations §3 for the planned refinement using SHRUG / Census B-04 district weighting.
Worker allocation: Event-weightedSidebar toggle. Districts with more heat events get proportionally more workers. Biases the worker base toward hot districts, pushing payouts up. Tests whether the uniform assumption is under-counting.
Worker allocation: Top 20% hotSidebar toggle. The hottest 20% of districts in a state get 60% of workers; other 80% of districts split 40%. A more extreme "workers concentrated in hot districts" stress-test. Usually produces the highest payout estimate.

Cap-hit summary at current tier, consec-days, payouts, and cap

A "district-year" is one district in one calendar year. The sample below contains ~7,020 district-years (695 districts × 10 years, 2015–2024). A district-year "hits the cap" when the uncapped per-worker payout (normal_events × ₹normal + severe_events × ₹severe) would exceed the seasonal cap. "Excess protected" = how much payout the cap prevents per-worker × workers-in-district, summed across hit district-years. This is the fund's savings from having the cap.

No cap vs 7-day vs 10-day 10-year national totals, apples-to-apples

This compares the three cap choices at the currently selected tier / consec-days / payout amounts. "No cap" = pay every event. "7-day cap" = Rs 4,600 per worker per season. "10-day cap" = Rs 6,550 per worker per season. The savings is how much the cap takes off the no-cap bill.

Blended funding scenario model a realistic deployment: split the annual premium across multiple sources

Scenarios 0/A/B/C/D in the left sidebar each assume ONE party covers the full premium gap. In reality, deployments typically blend sources — e.g., a donor underwrites the launch years, government picks up an increasing share as the scheme matures, and cess fills in for variance. Use the sliders below to model any blend. Total must sum to 100%.

Note: this tab does not modify the trigger rules, payout values, allocation, cap, or any other computation in the dashboard. It presents a pure premium-allocation view, using the same total annual premium as commercial-insurance scenarios (B/C/D) — i.e., expected loss × loading factor — and apportions it across funding sources according to the specified weights.
